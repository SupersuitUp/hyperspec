# Hand your first one-on-one to the person you manage

## Who sets the agenda

Your first one-on-one with a new report is the only meeting on your calendar where they should
set the agenda. Everything else you run. This one you hand over.

If you walk in with a list, you have told your report what the meeting is for, and it is for
you. They will answer your list politely and leave. You will know nothing you did not know when
you sat down, and so will they.

My team ran a [survey](materials/team-survey.md) this spring. 29 of 41 people said their most useful one-on-one in the last
quarter was one where they brought the first topic. The meeting that worked for them was the one
they started.

So give them the start. The simplest way to do it is a running agenda: one shared document per
person, kept for as long as you manage them. Your report adds items before each meeting, and you
add yours last, at the bottom. That is how an engineering manager I interviewed, eight years into
the job, runs hers. Her rule is short: the report owns the agenda.

## My first one-on-one

My first one-on-one as a manager was a disaster, and the reason was simple: I ran it. I had a
list, and I went down the list. Project status, blockers, the thing from Tuesday. Thirty minutes
later my report said thanks and left, and I had learned nothing I could not have read in the
tracker. Most new managers make the same mistake.

What I ran was a status meeting, which is a meeting spent reading out loud what the tracker
already says. Your report wrote those updates. Asking them to recite the updates to you teaches
you nothing new, and it spends the one half hour a week that belongs to them.

The survey show the cost from their side. 11 of 41 said at least one of their one-on-ones in the
last quarter was mostly project status. Read the tracker before you walk in, and leave status
there.

My second manager used to walk our one-on-ones outside, and I liked it. A walk takes the table
out of the room.

## The three questions

Here is what I ask now, in this order, and then I let the report take over:

1. What is taking more of your energy than it should?
2. What do you want to be doing more of in six months?
3. What should I stop doing, or start doing, that would make your week easier?

Three is enough to hand the meeting over. The first asks about this week. The second asks about
the next six months. The third asks about you, and it is the one your report will not raise
without being asked. A fourth question starts to look like your list again, and the list is what
you came to give up.

It can be helpful to consider asking the questions in the same words each time. This may help
your report become familiar with them, and over time they might start to prepare their answers.

The second question is the one my own team asked for. The most common free-text request in the
survey, in 9 responses, was to be asked what they want to work on next.

After the third question, stop talking.

## What to do with the answers

Wait after each question, longer than you want to. Dana, the engineering manager I interviewed,
puts it in three short sentences: "Wait. Count to five. The real answer is the second one." The first
answer your report gives is the tidy one, the version they could give anyone. The second is what
they came in with, and you only hear it if you let the silence run.

Write down what they say, in their words, at the top of the running agenda. That list is where
your next one-on-one starts, so the meeting stays theirs the week after too.

You might want to avoid trying to fix everything in the room. Pick one thing you can act on this week, say what you
will do, and do it before you meet again. The rest stays on the running agenda until it is done
or your report takes it off.

And keep your own urgent items out of it. She told me: "If I have something urgent, it is not a
one-on-one topic. I send it the day it happens." Your report should never have to wait a week to
hear something you needed them to know on Monday.

## Before the meeting

Open the invite and delete your list from it. Ask your report to add the first item to the
running agenda instead.

So write the three questions on a card. Ask the first one. Then wait, longer than feels polite,
because the first answer is the one they rehearsed and the second one is the one you came for.
